# 2026-08-30 — Distributed systems

*Sunday. Question and worked answer, both generated.*

---

Your team maintains a critical backend service responsible for managing inventory levels across multiple warehouses for a large e-commerce platform. When a customer places an order, the system needs to reserve the ordered items from available stock. Due to rapid growth, the `inventory` table now exceeds 50 million rows and is partitioned by `warehouse_id`.

A common operation is `reserve_stock(product_id, warehouse_id, quantity)`. This function is called frequently, sometimes thousands of times per second during peak sales events like Black Friday, and must ensure that stock is not oversold. The `inventory` table has columns for `product_id`, `warehouse_id`, and `available_quantity`.

The current implementation uses a single database transaction with `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` to decrement `available_quantity`. This approach is causing significant contention and locking issues on popular products in busy warehouses, leading to slow order processing and timeouts. The system needs to support high concurrency for reservations, ensure data consistency (no overselling), and maintain low latency (under 100ms for 99% of `reserve_stock` calls). Partial failures (e.g., a single warehouse's stock update failing) should not bring down the entire reservation process, and the system should gracefully handle retries.

## Requirements

Design an improved `reserve_stock` mechanism that minimizes contention on the main `inventory` table while ensuring stock consistency across high-volume reservations. Consider an event-driven or message-queue-based approach to decouple reservation requests from immediate database updates.

## Deliverables

1.  A high-level architecture diagram (text-based or conceptual description) illustrating the components involved in the new reservation flow and how they interact.
2.  A description of the mechanism used to guarantee that stock is not oversold, even under high concurrency and potential retries.
3.  An explanation of how eventual consistency or other consistency models are handled in your design, specifically detailing how the "truth" about inventory is maintained.
4.  A brief discussion on how your solution handles failures at different stages (e.g., message queue failure, worker processing failure, database update failure).

## Going further

Explain the trade-offs your chosen approach makes regarding immediate data consistency versus system throughput and latency, and under what specific conditions a stronger consistency model might be preferable, even at the cost of performance.

## Worked answer

### 1. High-level architecture diagram

The core idea is to move the immediate stock decrement out of the critical path of the order placement transaction and into an asynchronous, event-driven flow. This allows the order service to quickly acknowledge the reservation request while inventory updates are processed eventually. To prevent overselling, we introduce a mechanism that ensures only valid reservations are applied.

**Components:**

1.  **Order Placement Service (OPS):** The main service that receives customer order requests.
    *   **Function:** Validates order details, generates a unique `order_id` and `idempotency_key` for each reservation attempt, and publishes a `ReservationRequest` message to the message queue.
    *   **Database Interaction:** Updates the `orders` table with an initial status like `pending_inventory_confirmation`.
    *   **API:** Exposes the `POST /orders` endpoint.
2.  **Reservation Request Queue (e.g., Apache Kafka, AWS SQS):** A durable, highly available message queue.
    *   **Function:** Decouples the OPS from the Inventory Update Workers. Buffers reservation requests, handles backpressure, and ensures reliable delivery (at-least-once).
    *   **Partitioning:** Messages are typically partitioned by `warehouse_id` or `product_id` to ensure ordered processing for a given item/warehouse combination, minimizing contention within the worker group.
3.  **Inventory Update Worker Group:** A pool of stateless worker instances that consume messages from the `Reservation Request Queue`.
    *   **Function:** Processes `ReservationRequest` messages, attempts to decrement stock in the `inventory` table, and publishes `InventoryUpdateResult` messages.
    *   **Scalability:** Can be scaled horizontally based on queue depth and processing load.
4.  **Inventory Database (e.g., PostgreSQL, MySQL):** The primary source of truth for inventory levels. Partitioned by `warehouse_id`.
    *   **Tables:**
        *   `inventory`: Stores `product_id`, `warehouse_id`, `available_quantity`.
        *   `reservations_log`: A new table to track individual reservation attempts and their status.
5.  **Inventory Update Result Queue (e.g., Apache Kafka, AWS SQS):** Another message queue for worker results.
    *   **Function:** Publishes the outcome of each reservation attempt (success, failure, insufficient stock).
6.  **Order Status Update Service:** A service that consumes `InventoryUpdateResult` messages.
    *   **Function:** Updates the `order_status` in the `orders` table based on the inventory reservation outcome. If successful, changes status to `reserved` or `processing`. If failed, changes to `inventory_failed` and might trigger a compensation flow.
7.  **Order Database:** Stores `order_id`, `customer_id`, `items`, `order_status`.

**Flow:**

1.  Customer places an order via OPS.
2.  OPS publishes a `ReservationRequest` message to the queue, including `product_id`, `warehouse_id`, `quantity`, `order_id`, and a unique `idempotency_key`.
3.  OPS immediately responds to the customer with a `202 Accepted` status, indicating the order is `pending_inventory_confirmation`.
4.  An Inventory Update Worker consumes the `ReservationRequest` message.
5.  The worker attempts to reserve stock in the Inventory Database using a mechanism described in section 2.
6.  The worker publishes an `InventoryUpdateResult` message (success/failure) to the result queue.
7.  The Order Status Update Service consumes the `InventoryUpdateResult` and updates the `order_status` in the Order Database.
8.  (Optional) The Order Status Update Service can then trigger further downstream processes (e.g., payment capture, shipping).

### 2. Mechanism to guarantee stock is not oversold

To prevent overselling and handle high concurrency without `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` on the main `inventory` table, we introduce a `reservations_log` table and use an atomic update with a `CHECK` constraint or a `WHERE` clause condition.

**Database Schema Changes:**

We introduce a new table, `reservations_log`, which acts as a ledger for all reservation attempts.

```sql
-- Existing inventory table (simplified)
CREATE TABLE inventory (
    product_id UUID NOT NULL,
    warehouse_id UUID NOT NULL,
    available_quantity INT NOT NULL CHECK (available_quantity >= 0),
    PRIMARY KEY (product_id, warehouse_id)
) PARTITION BY HASH (warehouse_id); -- Example partitioning

-- New reservations_log table
CREATE TABLE reservations_log (
    reservation_id UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
    order_id UUID NOT NULL,
    product_id UUID NOT NULL,
    warehouse_id UUID NOT NULL,
    quantity INT NOT NULL CHECK (quantity > 0),
    status VARCHAR(20) NOT NULL DEFAULT 'pending', -- 'pending', 'reserved', 'failed', 'cancelled'
    idempotency_key UUID NOT NULL UNIQUE, -- Crucial for retries
    created_at TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE DEFAULT NOW(),
    updated_at TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE DEFAULT NOW(),
    -- Add indexes for efficient lookups
    INDEX idx_order_product_warehouse (order_id, product_id, warehouse_id),
    INDEX idx_product_warehouse_status (product_id, warehouse_id, status)
);
```

**Reservation Logic within an Inventory Update Worker:**

Each worker processes messages from the `Reservation Request Queue`. For each message:

1.  **Idempotency Check:** Before attempting any database operation, the worker checks if a reservation with the given `idempotency_key` (which is unique per `order_id` + `product_id` + `warehouse_id` combination) already exists in `reservations_log`. This prevents duplicate processing if a message is redelivered.

    ```sql
    -- Check for existing reservation
    SELECT status FROM reservations_log WHERE idempotency_key = :idempotency_key;
    -- If status is 'reserved' or 'failed', acknowledge message and exit.
    ```

2.  **Atomic Update with Conditional Decrement:** The core of the overselling prevention is a single, atomic SQL transaction that attempts to decrement `available_quantity` and record the reservation. This operation must be performed within a transaction.

    ```sql
    BEGIN;

    -- Attempt to insert into reservations_log first.
    -- This ensures we record the intent, even if inventory update fails.
    INSERT INTO reservations_log (order_id, product_id, warehouse_id, quantity, idempotency_key, status)
    VALUES (:order_id, :product_id, :warehouse_id, :quantity, :idempotency_key, 'pending')
    ON CONFLICT (idempotency_key) DO UPDATE SET updated_at = NOW() -- If idempotency_key exists, just update timestamp or ignore.
    RETURNING reservation_id;

    -- Attempt to decrement inventory. This is the critical step.
    -- The WHERE clause ensures available_quantity doesn't go below the requested quantity.
    UPDATE inventory
    SET available_quantity = available_quantity - :quantity
    WHERE product_id = :product_id AND warehouse_id = :warehouse_id AND available_quantity >= :quantity;

    -- Check if the UPDATE statement affected any rows.
    -- If no rows were affected, it means either the product/warehouse didn't exist,
    -- or available_quantity was less than :quantity.
    -- In PostgreSQL, you can check `ROW_COUNT()`.
    -- If ROW_COUNT() = 0, then the reservation failed due to insufficient stock.
    -- In this case, we update the reservations_log status to 'failed' and rollback.

    -- If UPDATE was successful (ROW_COUNT() = 1):
    UPDATE reservations_log
    SET status = 'reserved', updated_at = NOW()
    WHERE idempotency_key = :idempotency_key AND status = 'pending';

    COMMIT;
    ```

    **Explanation:**
    *   The `UPDATE inventory ... WHERE available_quantity >= :quantity` clause is crucial. The database's transaction isolation level (e.g., `READ COMMITTED` or `REPEATABLE READ` in PostgreSQL) guarantees that this check and decrement happen atomically. If multiple workers try to reserve the last item simultaneously, only one `UPDATE` will succeed because the `available_quantity` will drop below the threshold for subsequent transactions.
    *   By inserting into `reservations_log` first, we create a record of the attempt. If the `UPDATE inventory` fails, we can update the `reservations_log` entry to `failed` and publish a failure message.
    *   The `idempotency_key` ensures that even if a message is processed multiple times, the `INSERT` into `reservations_log` (and thus the subsequent `UPDATE inventory`) will only effectively happen once for that specific reservation request. `ON CONFLICT (idempotency_key) DO UPDATE` handles this gracefully.

This approach minimizes contention on the `inventory` table because:
*   `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` is eliminated.
*   The `UPDATE` statement is a direct decrement, which is highly optimized by the database.
*   The `WHERE` clause condition `available_quantity >= :quantity` acts as an optimistic lock, allowing many concurrent transactions to attempt updates. Only those that find sufficient stock will succeed.

### 3. How eventual consistency is handled and "truth" about inventory

This design embraces **eventual consistency** for the overall order status, but maintains **strong consistency** for the `available_quantity` in the `inventory` table itself.

*   **Strong Consistency for `inventory.available_quantity`:** The `inventory` table remains the single source of truth for the *actual* available stock. The atomic `UPDATE ... WHERE available_quantity >= :quantity` operation ensures that `available_quantity` is never decremented below zero and that no overselling occurs. This is a critical invariant that is always upheld.

*   **Eventual Consistency for Order Status:**
    *   When a customer places an order, the Order Placement Service immediately acknowledges it with a `pending_inventory_confirmation` status. At this point, the customer's order is not yet fully confirmed with reserved stock.
    *   The `order_status` in the Order Database will eventually transition to `reserved` (if stock was successfully reserved) or `inventory_failed` (if stock was insufficient or another error occurred). This transition happens asynchronously via the `Inventory Update Result Queue` and `Order Status Update Service`.
    *   The delay between the initial order placement and the final `order_status` update is the window of eventual consistency. During this time, the customer might see their order as "processing" or "awaiting stock confirmation."

*   **Maintaining the "Truth" about Inventory:**
    1.  **`inventory` table:** This table holds the current, strongly consistent count of physical stock available for immediate reservation. It is the definitive source for "how much can I sell right now?".
    2.  **`reservations_log` table:** This table serves as an audit log and a record of *pending* and *completed* reservations. It allows us to reconstruct the history of stock movements and understand why `available_quantity` changed. It also provides the `idempotency_key` to prevent double-counting.
        *   The sum of `quantity` for all `status = 'reserved'` entries for a given `product_id` and `warehouse_id` represents the total quantity *reserved* over time.
        *   The `available_quantity` in the `inventory` table should ideally be `initial_stock - SUM(quantity where status = 'reserved')`. Regular reconciliation jobs can verify this consistency.

**Implications for User Experience:**

*   Customers receive immediate feedback that their order request has been received.
*   They might see an "Order Processing" or "Awaiting Stock Confirmation" status for a short period (typically seconds to minutes, depending on queue processing latency).
*   If stock reservation fails, the customer is notified, and the order status is updated accordingly. This might trigger a refund or an offer for alternative products.

### 4. Handling failures at different stages

This event-driven architecture is inherently more resilient to partial failures compared to a tightly coupled transactional system.

1.  **Order Placement Service (OPS) Failure:**
    *   If OPS fails *before* publishing the `ReservationRequest` message, the order is not placed, and the customer receives an error.
    *   If OPS fails *after* publishing the message but *before* acknowledging the order to the customer, the message might be in the queue, but the order status in the Order Database is not updated. The customer might retry, leading to a new `order_id` but potentially the same `product_id`, `warehouse_id`, `quantity` combination. The `idempotency_key` helps prevent double-reservation if the original message eventually gets processed. The customer would likely see a timeout and retry.

2.  **Message Queue Failure (Reservation Request Queue or Inventory Update Result Queue):**
    *   **Durability:** Modern message queues (Kafka, SQS, RabbitMQ) are designed for high availability and durability. Messages are persisted to disk and replicated.
    *   **Producer Retries:** OPS (as a producer) should implement retry logic with exponential backoff if it fails to publish a message.
    *   **Consumer Retries:** Inventory Update Workers and Order Status Update Service (as consumers) will automatically retry processing messages if they fail to acknowledge them (e.g., due to worker crash). Messages remain in the queue until successfully processed and acknowledged.
    *   **Dead-Letter Queues (DLQs):** Messages that repeatedly fail processing after a configured number of retries are moved to a DLQ. This prevents poison pills from blocking the queue and allows manual inspection and reprocessing.

3.  **Inventory Update Worker Processing Failure:**
    *   **Worker Crash/Restart:** If a worker crashes while processing a message, the message will not be acknowledged. The message queue will redeliver it to another available worker (or the same worker after restart). The `idempotency_key` in the `reservations_log` table is critical here to ensure that the stock decrement is not applied multiple times if the database transaction partially completed before the crash.
    *   **Database Connection Issues:** The worker should implement retry logic for database operations with exponential backoff.
    *   **Insufficient Stock:** This is a business logic "failure," not a system failure. The worker will detect this (e.g., `ROW_COUNT() = 0` for the `UPDATE inventory` statement), mark the `reservations_log` entry as `failed`, and publish an `InventoryUpdateResult` indicating `INSUFFICIENT_STOCK`. This allows the Order Status Update Service to update the order accordingly.
    *   **Poison Pills:** If a message is malformed or consistently causes an error, it will eventually be moved to a DLQ.

4.  **Inventory Database Update Failure:**
    *   **Transaction Atomicity:** The reservation logic within the worker is wrapped in a transaction. If any part of the transaction fails (e.g., database constraint violation, disk full), the entire transaction is rolled back, ensuring no partial updates.
    *   **Database Unavailability:** If the database is completely unavailable, workers will fail to process messages. Messages will remain in the queue and be retried. This will cause a backlog in the `Reservation Request Queue` and increased latency for order confirmations. Monitoring queue depth is crucial to detect this.
    *   **Partition Failure:** If one `warehouse_id` partition of the `inventory` table is unavailable, only reservations for that specific warehouse will be affected. Reservations for other warehouses can continue processing normally, demonstrating the benefit of partitioning.

### Going further

The chosen approach makes a fundamental trade-off: **immediate data consistency for order status is sacrificed for higher system throughput and lower latency for order placement.**

**Trade-offs Accepted:**

*   **Eventual Consistency for Order Status:** The customer's order status is not immediately `reserved` upon placement. There is a delay (from milliseconds to potentially minutes during extreme load or failures) before the inventory is confirmed and the order status is updated. This means the customer might see a "pending" status for a short period.
*   **Increased System Complexity:** Introducing message queues, worker services, and an additional `reservations_log` table adds complexity to the architecture, monitoring, and debugging compared to a single monolithic transaction.
*   **Potential for Compensation:** If a reservation fails after the order is initially accepted (e.g., due to insufficient stock), a compensation mechanism (e.g., refund, alternative product offer) is required, which adds complexity to the business logic.
*   **Read-Your-Own-Writes Consistency:** A customer might place an order, then immediately check its status and see `pending_inventory_confirmation` even though the reservation message has already been sent. This is a common eventual consistency pattern.

**Benefits Gained:**

*   **High Throughput for Order Placement:** The Order Placement Service can process thousands of orders per second by simply publishing a message to a queue, without waiting for slow database locks. This directly addresses the "thousands of times per second" requirement.
*   **Low Latency for Order Acknowledgment:** Customers receive a `202 Accepted` response quickly, improving user experience by reducing perceived latency.
*   **Reduced Database Contention:** The `inventory` table experiences fewer, shorter, and more efficient `UPDATE` operations, as `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` is removed. Contention is distributed across partitions and handled by the database's optimistic concurrency control.
*   **Increased Resilience:** Decoupling components via message queues makes the system more resilient to individual component failures. A worker crash doesn't block order placement.
*   **Scalability:** Both the Order Placement Service and Inventory Update Workers can be scaled independently.

**Conditions where a stronger consistency model might be preferable:**

A stronger consistency model, such as **strong consistency** (where all reads return the most recent write, and all writes are immediately visible globally), might be preferable in scenarios where:

1.  **Financial Transactions or Critical Resource Allocation:** For highly sensitive operations where even a momentary inconsistency could lead to significant financial loss or safety issues.
    *   *Example:* Allocating a limited number of high-value concert tickets or airline seats where double-booking is absolutely unacceptable, and the cost of a failed reservation is very high (e.g., legal implications, customer trust). In such cases, the system might need to block until a definitive reservation is confirmed, even if it means higher latency.
2.  **Real-time Inventory Display for Very Scarce Items:** If displaying the *exact* real-time available quantity to customers is paramount for extremely limited, high-demand products (e.g., limited edition sneakers), waiting for eventual consistency might lead to a poor user experience where an item appears available but is then found to be out of stock during checkout.
    *   *However, even here, many systems opt for eventual consistency with a "last-mile" check at payment.*
3.  **Simpler System Requirements / Lower Scale:** For systems with lower transaction volumes (e.g., tens or hundreds of reservations per second, not thousands), the overhead of an event-driven architecture might outweigh the benefits. A simpler, strongly consistent transactional model (even with `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`) might suffice and be easier to maintain.
4.  **Strict Legal or Regulatory Compliance:** Certain industries might have regulations that mandate immediate and verifiable consistency for specific data points.

In our e-commerce inventory scenario, the chosen eventual consistency model for order status is generally acceptable because:
*   The `inventory` table itself maintains strong consistency, preventing overselling.
*   Customers are accustomed to "processing" order statuses.
*   The primary goal is high throughput and low latency for the *initial* order placement, which is achieved. The cost of a failed reservation (e.g., refund, apology) is typically lower than the cost of losing many sales due to system bottlenecks.
