## Scenario

Your team is responsible for a mission-critical backend service that processes real-time transaction data for a large financial technology platform. One of the core functionalities is to securely store and rapidly retrieve **audit logs** for every financial transaction. These logs are critical for regulatory compliance and dispute resolution.

Currently, these audit logs are being written directly to your main PostgreSQL database. The `audit_logs` table has grown to hundreds of millions of rows, with each transaction generating 1-5 log entries. During peak hours, the write load on this table can exceed 5,000 writes per second, leading to occasional database contention and increased latency for other critical operations on the primary database. Additionally, historical audit logs (older than 90 days) are rarely accessed, but must be retained for 7 years. Retrieving these older logs for compliance checks can be slow and resource-intensive on the active database.

You've been tasked with designing and implementing a solution to offload the storage and retrieval of these audit logs from the primary PostgreSQL database, while maintaining strict data integrity and compliance requirements.

## Requirements

The solution must:
*   **Decouple Write Load:** Significantly reduce the write load on the primary PostgreSQL database for audit log entries.
*   **Scalable Storage:** Provide a highly scalable and durable storage solution capable of handling current and future growth of audit logs (expecting billions of entries over time).
*   **Cost-Effective Retention:** Store historical audit logs (older than 90 days) in a cost-effective manner, optimized for infrequent access.
*   **Retrieval Performance:** Allow for efficient retrieval of both recent (last 90 days) and historical audit logs, with recent logs needing to be accessible within 1-2 seconds, and historical logs within 10-15 seconds.
*   **Data Integrity:** Ensure that no audit log entries are lost during ingestion or storage.

## Deliverables

1.  A high-level architectural diagram (can be text-based or ASCII art) outlining the AWS services chosen and their interactions for the new audit log pipeline and storage.
2.  A brief written justification for the primary AWS services selected, explaining why they meet the requirements (scalability, durability, cost-effectiveness, performance).
3.  Describe the strategy for handling the transition of existing audit logs from PostgreSQL to the new system, specifically for logs older than 90 days.
4.  Outline a monitoring approach to ensure the new audit log pipeline is healthy and data integrity is maintained.

## Going further

Consider a scenario where an upstream service occasionally sends duplicate audit log entries due to retry mechanisms. How would your chosen architecture prevent or gracefully handle these duplicates to maintain data integrity and prevent data bloat, without introducing significant latency?

### 2. Justification for primary AWS services

*   **AWS Kinesis Data Firehose:**
    *   **Decouple Write Load & Scalable Ingestion:** Firehose acts as a fully managed, highly scalable buffer. The backend service writes to Firehose, not directly to storage. Firehose can handle thousands of writes per second and automatically scales to match incoming data volume. It batches records and delivers them to S3, significantly reducing the number of individual write operations on the storage layer. This completely decouples the primary database from audit log writes.
    *   **Data Integrity:** Firehose provides at-least-once delivery semantics. It buffers data and retries delivery to S3, ensuring no data loss even if S3 is temporarily unavailable.
    *   **Simplicity:** It's easier to integrate with than Kinesis Data Streams for simple fan-out to S3, as it handles scaling, sharding, and error handling automatically.

*   **AWS S3 (Simple Storage Service):**
    *   **Scalable Storage & Durability:** S3 offers virtually unlimited storage capacity and 99.999999999% (11 nines) durability, making it ideal for storing billions of audit log entries reliably. It's the foundational storage for both raw and processed logs.
    *   **Cost-Effective Retention:** S3 provides various storage classes. For historical logs, we can leverage S3 Intelligent-Tiering, S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval, or S3 Glacier Deep Archive. S3 lifecycle policies can automatically transition objects to cheaper tiers after 90 days, optimizing costs for infrequently accessed data.
    *   **Foundation for Analytics:** S3 is a common data lake foundation, allowing services like Athena to query data directly in place.

*   **AWS Lambda:**
    *   **Data Processing & Transformation:** Lambda functions are event-driven and serverless, perfect for processing S3 events when new log files arrive. This allows for parsing, validation, enrichment, and deduplication of logs before indexing or final storage. It scales automatically with the volume of incoming S3 objects.
    *   **Decoupling:** It processes data asynchronously, preventing backpressure on the ingestion pipeline.

*   **AWS OpenSearch Service:**
    *   **Retrieval Performance (Recent Logs):** OpenSearch is a managed search and analytics engine, providing fast, full-text search capabilities. It's ideal for interactive queries of recent logs (last 90 days) with low latency (1-2 seconds). Its distributed nature allows it to scale for high query loads and large data volumes.
    *   **Operational Visibility:** Provides a Kibana interface for easy exploration and visualization of logs by operations teams.

*   **AWS Athena:**
    *   **Retrieval Performance (Historical Logs) & Cost-Effectiveness:** Athena is a serverless interactive query service that makes it easy to analyze data directly in S3 using standard SQL. It's highly cost-effective for infrequent historical queries because you only pay for the data scanned. It can query petabytes of data, making it suitable for billions of historical log entries, with typical query times of 10-15 seconds for complex queries over large datasets.
    *   **No Infrastructure Management:** As a serverless service, there's no infrastructure to provision or manage.

### 3. Strategy for transitioning existing audit logs

The transition of existing audit logs from PostgreSQL to the new system, specifically for logs older than 90 days, will involve a phased approach to minimize impact on the primary database and ensure data integrity.

1.  **Identify and Isolate Historical Data:**
    *   First, identify all `audit_logs` records in PostgreSQL that are older than 90 days. This will be the target dataset for migration.
    *   Consider creating a read replica of the PostgreSQL database if possible, and perform the export from the replica to minimize load on the primary.

2.  **Export Data in Batches:**
    *   Export the historical data from PostgreSQL in manageable batches. This prevents overwhelming the database or the export process. A good batch size might be based on `id` ranges or `timestamp` ranges (e.g., one month's worth of data at a time).
    *   The export format should be a structured, self-describing format like JSON Lines or Apache Parquet. Parquet is highly recommended for its columnar storage, compression, and schema evolution capabilities, which are beneficial for later querying with Athena.

    Example SQL for batch export (conceptual):
    ```sql
    COPY (
        SELECT
            id,
            transaction_id,
            event_type,
            event_timestamp,
            actor_id,
            details_json,
            -- ... other columns
        FROM audit_logs
        WHERE event_timestamp < NOW() - INTERVAL '90 days'
        ORDER BY event_timestamp
        LIMIT 100000 OFFSET <offset_value>
    ) TO '/tmp/audit_logs_batch_YYYYMMDD_HHMMSS.jsonl';
    ```

3.  **Upload to S3:**
    *   Upload the exported batches directly to the `s3://my-app-audit-logs/processed/historical/` prefix.
    *   Organize the data in S3 using a Hive-style partitioning scheme based on `event_timestamp` (e.g., `s3://my-app-audit-logs/processed/historical/year=YYYY/month=MM/day=DD/`). This is crucial for efficient querying with Athena, as it allows Athena to prune partitions and scan less data.

    Example S3 path:
    `s3://my-app-audit-logs/processed/historical/year=2023/month=01/day=15/batch_001.parquet`

4.  **Create Athena Table:**
    *   Once data is in S3, define an external table in AWS Glue Data Catalog (which Athena uses) that points to the `s3://my-app-audit-logs/processed/historical/` prefix and recognizes the Parquet format and partitioning scheme.

    Example Athena DDL:
    ```sql
    CREATE EXTERNAL TABLE IF NOT EXISTS historical_audit_logs (
      id BIGINT,
      transaction_id STRING,
      event_type STRING,
      event_timestamp TIMESTAMP,
      actor_id STRING,
      details_json STRING
      -- ... other columns matching your schema
    )
    PARTITIONED BY (
      year INT,
      month INT,
      day INT
    )
    ROW FORMAT SERDE 'org.apache.hadoop.hive.ql.io.parquet.serde.ParquetHiveSerDe'
    STORED AS INPUTFORMAT 'org.apache.hadoop.hive.ql.io.parquet.MapredParquetInputFormat'
    OUTPUTFORMAT 'org.apache.hadoop.hive.ql.io.parquet.MapredParquetOutputFormat'
    LOCATION 's3://my-app-audit-logs/processed/historical/';

    -- After uploading each batch, run MSCK REPAIR TABLE to discover new partitions
    ALTER TABLE historical_audit_logs REPAIR TABLE;
    ```

5.  **Validation:**
    *   After each batch upload and Athena table update, perform spot checks by querying Athena to ensure data integrity and accessibility. Compare row counts or specific record details against the source PostgreSQL database.

6.  **Decommissioning (Optional, but recommended):**
    *   Once all historical data is successfully migrated and validated in S3/Athena, and the new real-time pipeline is fully operational, consider archiving or deleting the corresponding old records from the primary PostgreSQL `audit_logs` table to reclaim space and improve performance. This should be done cautiously and after thorough verification.

### 4. Monitoring approach

A robust monitoring approach is critical to ensure the health, performance, and data integrity of the new audit log pipeline.

1.  **AWS Kinesis Data Firehose:**
    *   **Metrics (CloudWatch):**
        *   `IncomingBytes`, `IncomingRecords`: Monitor the volume of data flowing into Firehose. Spikes or drops indicate issues with the upstream service.
        *   `DeliveryToS3.Bytes`, `DeliveryToS3.Records`: Monitor successful delivery to S3.
        *   `DeliveryToS3.DataFreshness`: Ensure data is being delivered to S3 promptly.
        *   `DeliveryToS3.RecordsBlocked`, `DeliveryToS3.BytesBlocked`: Indicate issues preventing delivery to S3 (e.g., permissions, S3 unavailability).
        *   `ThrottledPutRecords`: Indicates if the backend service is sending data faster than Firehose can accept (though Firehose is highly scalable, it has limits).
    *   **Alarms:** Set alarms on `DeliveryToS3.RecordsBlocked` > 0, `DeliveryToS3.DataFreshness` > 5 minutes, or significant drops in `IncomingRecords`.

2.  **AWS S3 (Raw and Processed Buckets):**
    *   **Metrics (CloudWatch):**
        *   `BucketSizeBytes`, `NumberOfObjects`: Monitor overall storage growth.
        *   `PutRequests`, `GetRequests`: Monitor access patterns.
    *   **S3 Event Notifications:** Ensure the Lambda function is successfully triggered for new objects in the raw bucket. Monitor Lambda invocation metrics.
    *   **S3 Inventory Reports:** Periodically generate reports to verify object counts and sizes, and to ensure lifecycle policies are correctly applied.
    *   **Alarms:** On `Errors` for S3 Put/Get requests if unexpected.

3.  **AWS Lambda (Log Processor):**
    *   **Metrics (CloudWatch):**
        *   `Invocations`: Ensure the function is being triggered as expected.
        *   `Errors`: Monitor for processing failures (e.g., parsing errors, OpenSearch connection issues).
        *   `Duration`: Monitor execution time to detect performance bottlenecks.
        *   `Throttles`: Indicates if Lambda is being invoked too frequently or hitting concurrency limits.
        *   `DeadLetterErrors`: If a Dead Letter Queue (DLQ) is configured, monitor errors when sending failed events to the DLQ.
    *   **Logs (CloudWatch Logs):**
        *   Monitor Lambda logs for specific error messages, malformed log entries, or issues during OpenSearch indexing or S3 writes. Implement structured logging for easier analysis.
    *   **Alarms:** Set alarms on `Errors` > 0, `Throttles` > 0, or `Duration` exceeding a threshold.

4.  **AWS OpenSearch Service:**
    *   **Metrics (CloudWatch):**
        *   `CPUUtilization`, `JVMMemoryPressure`, `DiskQueueDepth`: Monitor cluster health and resource utilization.
        *   `IndexingRate`, `SearchRate`: Monitor data ingestion and query performance.
        *   `ClusterStatus.red`, `ClusterStatus.yellow`: Critical alarms for cluster health.
        *   `FreeStorageSpace`: Monitor disk usage to prevent capacity issues.
    *   **Logs (CloudWatch Logs):**
        *   OpenSearch error logs for indexing failures or query issues.
    *   **Alarms:** Alarms on `ClusterStatus.red` or `yellow`, high `JVMMemoryPressure`, low `FreeStorageSpace`, or significant drops in `IndexingRate`.

5.  **AWS Athena:**
    *   **Metrics (CloudWatch):**
        *   `QueryCount`: Monitor the number of queries executed.
        *   `BytesScanned`: Monitor the amount of data scanned, which directly impacts cost. High `BytesScanned` for simple queries might indicate inefficient partitioning or table definitions.
        *   `FailedQueryCount`: Monitor for query failures.
    *   **Logs (CloudWatch Logs):**
        *   Athena query history and execution details for debugging slow or failed queries.
    *   **Alarms:** On `FailedQueryCount` > 0.

6.  **Data Integrity Checks:**
    *   **End-to-End Reconciliation:** Implement a periodic (e.g., daily) reconciliation process.
        *   Compare the number of records ingested into Firehose (from `IncomingRecords` metric) with the number of records processed by Lambda and indexed in OpenSearch, and the number of objects written to S3.
        *   Use a unique identifier (e.g., `transaction_id` + `event_timestamp` + `event_type`) to sample and verify a small percentage of records across the pipeline, ensuring they match the source and destination.
    *   **Checksums/Hashes:** If possible, include a hash of the log entry content at the source. The Lambda processor can re-calculate and verify this hash to detect accidental corruption during transit.
    *   **DLQ Monitoring:** Ensure the Lambda's Dead Letter Queue (DLQ) is monitored. Any messages in the DLQ indicate processing failures that require investigation and manual intervention.

7.  **Application-level Monitoring:**
    *   The backend service itself should emit metrics for the number of audit logs successfully published to Kinesis Firehose. This provides an upstream view of the pipeline's input.

### Going further: Handling duplicate audit log entries

The chosen architecture can gracefully handle duplicate audit log entries primarily within the AWS Lambda log processor, before data is indexed in OpenSearch or permanently stored in S3.

**Strategy: Deduplication using a combination of unique identifiers and a short-term store.**

1.  **Define a Unique Identifier:**
    Each audit log entry must have a deterministic, immutable unique identifier. This ID should be composed of fields that, together, uniquely identify a single logical audit event. For example:
    `unique_id = HASH(transaction_id + event_type + event_timestamp + actor_id + details_json_hash)`
    The `details_json_hash` is important if the `details_json` can vary slightly but still represent the same logical event, or if the `details_json` itself is part of the uniqueness criteria.

2.  **Lambda Log Processor with DynamoDB for Deduplication:**

    *   **Service:** AWS DynamoDB (for its low-latency key-value store capabilities).
    *   **Mechanism:**
        1.  When the Lambda function receives a batch of log entries from S3 (triggered by Firehose delivery), for each log entry, it first computes its `unique_id`.
        2.  It then attempts to write this `unique_id` to a DynamoDB table (e.g., `audit_log_dedup_cache`) with a short Time-To-Live (TTL). The TTL should be configured to be slightly longer than the maximum expected retry window for upstream services (e.g., 5-10 minutes).
        3.  The DynamoDB write operation will use a conditional write, specifically `attribute_not_exists(id)`.

    **DynamoDB Table Schema (conceptual):**
    *   `id` (Primary Key, String): Stores the `unique_id` computed from the log entry.
    *   `ttl` (Number): Unix timestamp indicating when the item should expire.

    **Lambda Deduplication Logic (pseudo-code):**

    ```python
    import json
    import hashlib
    import time
    import os
    import boto3

    DYNAMODB_TABLE_NAME = os.environ.get('DYNAMODB_TABLE_NAME', 'audit_log_dedup_cache')
    DYNAMODB_TTL_SECONDS = int(os.environ.get('DYNAMODB_TTL_SECONDS', 600)) # 10 minutes
    dynamodb = boto3.resource('dynamodb')
    table = dynamodb.Table(DYNAMODB_TABLE_NAME)

    def generate_unique_id(log_entry):
        # Ensure consistent ordering of keys for hashing
        sorted_keys = sorted(log_entry.keys())
        hash_input = ''.join(str(log_entry[k]) for k in sorted_keys)
        return hashlib.sha256(hash_input.encode('utf-8')).hexdigest()

    def lambda_handler(event, context):
        processed_records = []
        for record in event['Records']:
            # Assuming S3 event, parse the S3 object
            bucket = record['s3']['bucket']['name']
            key = record['s3']['object']['key']
            s3_object = boto3.client('s3').get_object(Bucket=bucket, Key=key)
            log_data = s3_object['Body'].read().decode('utf-8')

            # Assuming log_data is JSON Lines
            for line in log_data.splitlines():
                try:
                    log_entry = json.loads(line)
                    unique_id = generate_unique_id(log_entry)
                    
                    # Attempt to write to DynamoDB with conditional check
                    current_time = int(time.time())
                    ttl_expiry = current_time + DYNAMODB_TTL_SECONDS

                    try:
                        table.put_item(
                            Item={
                                'id': unique_id,
                                'ttl': ttl_expiry
                            },
                            ConditionExpression='attribute_not_exists(id)'
                        )
                        # If put_item succeeds, it's a new unique log entry
                        processed_records.append(log_entry)
                    except table.meta.client.exceptions.ConditionalCheckFailedException:
                        # Item already exists, this is a duplicate. Skip it.
                        print(f"Duplicate log entry detected and skipped: {unique_id}")
                        continue # Skip to the next log entry
                    
                except json.JSONDecodeError as e:
                    print(f"Error decoding JSON: {e} - {line}")
                    # Handle malformed log entries, e.g., send to DLQ
                except Exception as e:
                    print(f"Unexpected error processing log entry: {e} - {line}")
                    # Handle other errors

        # Continue with processing unique_records (e.g., index to OpenSearch, write to S3 processed)
        # ...
        return {
            'statusCode': 200,
            'body': f'Processed {len(processed_records)} unique records.'
        }
    ```

**Why this works and trade-offs:**

*   **Prevents Data Bloat:** Duplicates are identified and discarded early in the pipeline, preventing them from being indexed in OpenSearch (saving indexing costs and storage) and from being stored as separate entries in the processed S3 bucket (saving storage).
*   **Maintains Data Integrity:** Only truly unique logical audit events are retained, ensuring that compliance and dispute resolution processes rely on accurate, non-duplicated data.
*   **Low Latency Impact:** DynamoDB reads/writes for a single item are typically in the single-digit milliseconds. For batch processing in Lambda, this adds a small, acceptable overhead per log entry. DynamoDB is highly scalable and can handle the throughput required.
*   **Cost-Effective:** DynamoDB with TTL is cost-effective for this use case. Items are automatically deleted after their TTL, so the table doesn't grow indefinitely, and you only pay for the storage and operations for the active deduplication window.
*   **Graceful Handling:** Duplicates are simply skipped, not treated as errors that halt the pipeline.

**Trade-offs:**

*   **Eventual Consistency Window:** The deduplication relies on the `unique_id` being present in DynamoDB within the TTL window. If a duplicate arrives *after* the TTL has expired (e.g., a very delayed retry), it might be treated as a new entry. However, for typical retry mechanisms, a 5-10 minute TTL is usually sufficient.
*   **DynamoDB Cost:** There's an additional cost for DynamoDB operations and storage. This needs to be factored into the overall solution cost.
*   **Complexity:** Introduces an additional service (DynamoDB) and logic into the Lambda function, increasing the complexity of the pipeline.
*   **Hashing Overhead:** Generating a hash for each log entry adds a small computational overhead in the Lambda function. The choice of fields for the `unique_id` is critical; too few fields might lead to false negatives (treating distinct events as duplicates), too many or highly variable fields might make hashing inefficient or miss true duplicates.

This approach effectively addresses the "going further" scenario by providing a robust, scalable, and relatively low-latency mechanism to prevent duplicate audit log entries from polluting the system.
