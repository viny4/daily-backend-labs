## Scenario

Your team manages a critical backend service responsible for delivering time-sensitive marketing notifications (e.g., "Your cart expires in 1 hour," "New product in your wishlist") to millions of users across a global platform. These notifications are scheduled by an upstream service, which places messages into a queue. Your service consumes these messages, determines the appropriate delivery channel (email, push notification, in-app), and dispatches them.

The problem is that the notification volume peaks significantly during promotional events (e.g., Black Friday, major product launches), often reaching several hundred thousand notifications per minute for sustained periods. During these peaks, you've observed a substantial increase in notification delivery latency, with some notifications arriving hours late or even failing entirely due to processing backlogs. The current single-region, queue-based architecture is struggling to keep up, leading to a poor user experience and missed marketing opportunities.

You suspect that the core issue is not just the raw message processing rate, but also the potential for "hot spots" – specific users or notification types that generate a disproportionate number of messages and can overwhelm individual workers or downstream delivery services if not handled carefully. Additionally, the system needs to be resilient to transient failures of individual delivery channels or worker nodes without losing notifications.

## Requirements

Design a more robust and scalable architecture for this notification delivery service. Your solution should explicitly address the challenges of high throughput, variable load, hot spots, and resilience to partial failures. The design should leverage distributed systems principles to ensure notifications are processed and delivered within a 5-minute latency budget, even during peak loads.

Constraints:
*   Average daily volume: 50 million notifications.
*   Peak volume: 300,000 notifications per minute for up to 4 hours.
*   Latency budget: 99% of notifications must be delivered within 5 minutes of being scheduled.
*   No single point of failure.
*   Notifications must be delivered exactly once per user for a given event (e.g., a cart expiry notification should not be sent twice).
*   The upstream scheduling service cannot be modified; it will continue to send messages to a single conceptual queue.

## Deliverables

1.  A high-level architectural diagram (can be text-based using ASCII art or described in detail) illustrating the components and data flow of your proposed solution.
2.  A brief explanation of how your design addresses the "hot spots" problem.
3.  An outline of the mechanisms used to ensure "exactly once" delivery semantics for notifications, considering potential retries and failures.
4.  A description of how your system handles the failure of an individual worker node or a downstream delivery channel (e.g., email API is temporarily down).

## Going further

Explain the trade-offs involved in choosing your specific approach for handling hot spots versus a simpler round-robin distribution strategy. Under what circumstances would your chosen approach become problematic, and what alternative would you consider then?

**Data Flow Explanation:**

1.  **Upstream Scheduling Service:** Sends notification requests to a single conceptual endpoint, which is handled by our Ingestion Service.
2.  **Ingestion Service:** Acts as an API Gateway or Load Balancer. It receives messages, performs basic validation, and routes them to the appropriate partition in the Distributed Message Queue. This service is responsible for determining the partitioning key (e.g., `user_id` or a combination of `user_id` and `event_type`).
3.  **Distributed Message Queue (Kafka/Kinesis):** This is the backbone for high-throughput, durable message storage. Messages are partitioned to allow parallel processing and prevent hot spots. Each partition is an ordered, immutable sequence of records.
4.  **Notification Processing Service:** A fleet of stateless worker instances that consume messages from the Distributed Message Queue. These workers perform the core logic:
    *   **Enrichment:** Fetching user preferences (e.g., preferred delivery channel, language) from a data store (not shown for brevity, but could be a database or another cache).
    *   **Deduplication:** Checking the Distributed Cache to ensure a notification for a specific user/event hasn't already been processed.
    *   **Rate Limiting:** Applying per-user or per-channel rate limits, using counters in the Distributed Cache.
    *   **Channel Selection:** Determining the optimal delivery channel based on preferences and availability.
    *   **Dispatch:** Placing the processed notification into the appropriate Outbound Message Queue.
5.  **Distributed Cache (Redis Cluster):** Used for fast lookups of deduplication state, rate limit counters, and potentially frequently accessed user preferences. Its clustered nature ensures high availability and scalability.
6.  **Outbound Message Queues (SQS/Kafka):** Dedicated queues for each downstream delivery channel (Email, Push, In-App). This decouples the processing service from the delivery services, allowing independent scaling and retries.
7.  **Delivery Services (Email, Push, In-App):** External services responsible for the actual delivery of notifications. They consume from their respective outbound queues.

### 2. How the design addresses the "hot spots" problem

The primary mechanism for addressing hot spots is **message partitioning** in the Distributed Message Queue (e.g., Kafka or Kinesis).

When the Ingestion Service receives a notification, it computes a **partitioning key**. A good candidate for this key is `user_id`. If a specific `user_id` generates a disproportionate number of messages, all messages for that user will land in the same partition. While this might seem counter-intuitive, it ensures that:

1.  **Ordering per user:** All notifications for a single user are processed in the order they were scheduled, preventing race conditions (e.g., a "cart expires" notification being sent after a "cart purchased" notification).
2.  **Isolation of hot users:** A single "hot user" will only impact the processing of their own partition. Other partitions, handling other users, can continue to be processed in parallel by other worker instances without being blocked.
3.  **Scalability:** The Notification Processing Service can scale by adding more worker instances, each consuming from one or more partitions. If a partition becomes a bottleneck due to a hot user, more resources can be allocated to the workers consuming that specific partition, or the partitioning strategy can be refined.

**Example Partitioning Logic (Conceptual):**

```
// Ingestion Service pseudo-code
function routeNotification(notification):
    partition_key = notification.user_id % NUM_PARTITIONS // Simple modulo hashing
    // Or a more sophisticated consistent hashing algorithm
    // partition_key = consistent_hash(notification.user_id)

    send_to_queue_partition(notification, partition_key)
```

For extremely hot *event types* (e.g., a "flash sale starts now" notification sent to millions simultaneously), the `user_id` partitioning still works well because each user's notification is distinct. However, if the hot spot is due to a single *type* of notification overwhelming a *downstream delivery service*, the outbound message queues (one per channel) help isolate this. For instance, if the Email Service is slow, it won't impact Push or In-App notifications.

### 3. Mechanisms for "exactly once" delivery semantics

Achieving true "exactly once" delivery in a distributed system is notoriously hard and often translates to "effectively once" or "at least once with strong deduplication." Our design aims for "effectively once" per user for a given event, which is sufficient for marketing notifications.

Here's how it's achieved:

1.  **Idempotent Message Processing:**
    *   Each notification message from the upstream service should ideally contain a unique identifier for the event it represents (e.g., `notification_id`, `cart_expiry_event_id`, `product_launch_id`). This ID, combined with `user_id`, forms a natural **deduplication key**.
    *   When a Notification Processing Service worker consumes a message, before doing any significant work or dispatching, it attempts to record the processing of this unique event for this user.

2.  **Distributed Cache for Deduplication State:**
    *   The Notification Processing Service uses a Distributed Cache (e.g., Redis) to store the state of processed notifications.
    *   Before processing a notification (`user_id`, `event_id`), the worker attempts to set a key in Redis.

    **Redis Command Example:**
    ```
    SET notification_status:{user_id}:{event_id} "processed" NX EX 3600
    ```
    *   `SET ... NX`: This command attempts to set the key *only if it does not already exist* (`NX`). If the key already exists, the command returns `nil`, indicating that another worker (or a retry of the same worker) has already processed this specific notification for this user.
    *   `EX 3600`: Sets an expiry time (e.g., 1 hour). This is important to prevent the cache from growing indefinitely and to allow for re-sending if the event is genuinely re-triggered after a long period. The expiry should be longer than any expected retry window.

3.  **Consumer Group Offsets (Kafka/Kinesis):**
    *   The Distributed Message Queue (Kafka/Kinesis) maintains consumer offsets. Workers commit their offsets *only after* a message has been successfully processed and its deduplication state recorded in Redis. This ensures that if a worker fails *before* committing, the message will be re-delivered to another worker (or the same worker upon restart). The deduplication logic then prevents duplicate delivery.

4.  **Idempotent Downstream Delivery Services (Best Effort):**
    *   While our service ensures "effectively once" dispatch to the outbound queues, the downstream delivery services (Email, Push, In-App) should ideally also be designed to be idempotent. They should use the `notification_id` or `event_id` passed along to them to prevent sending the same email twice, for example. This is outside the scope of *our* service but is a crucial part of the end-to-end "exactly once" goal.

**Flow for "Exactly Once":**

1.  Worker consumes message `M` with `user_id` and `event_id`.
2.  Worker attempts `SET notification_status:{user_id}:{event_id} "processed" NX EX 3600` in Redis.
3.  **If `SET` returns `nil`:** The notification has already been processed (or is currently being processed by another worker). The worker discards `M` and commits its offset for `M`.
4.  **If `SET` returns `OK`:** The notification is being processed for the first time.
    *   Perform enrichment, rate limiting, channel selection.
    *   Place the notification into the appropriate Outbound Message Queue.
    *   Commit the offset for `M` in the Distributed Message Queue.

This approach handles worker crashes, network issues, and retries gracefully, ensuring that even if a message is processed multiple times from the input queue, it results in only one actual dispatch to a downstream delivery channel.

### 4. Handling failures

#### a. Failure of an individual worker node

1.  **Heartbeating and Liveness Checks:** Worker nodes in the Notification Processing Service are typically part of an auto-scaling group or managed by an orchestrator (e.g., Kubernetes). These systems perform health checks. If a worker fails (crashes, becomes unresponsive), it's detected.
2.  **Distributed Message Queue Rebalancing:**
    *   When a worker consuming from Kafka/Kinesis fails, its connection to the message queue is lost.
    *   The message queue system detects this and automatically triggers a **rebalance** of the consumer group.
    *   The partitions previously assigned to the failed worker are re-assigned to other healthy workers in the same consumer group.
3.  **Uncommitted Messages:** Any messages that the failed worker had consumed but *not yet committed* (because processing wasn't complete or the deduplication state wasn't recorded) will be re-delivered to the newly assigned workers.
4.  **Deduplication Handles Retries:** The "exactly once" mechanism (using Redis `SET ... NX`) ensures that even if a message is re-delivered and processed by a different worker, it won't result in a duplicate notification being sent if it was already successfully dispatched before the original worker failed. The new worker will attempt the `SET ... NX` command, find the key already exists, and discard the message.

This combination ensures that failures are transparently handled, and processing resumes with minimal interruption, without losing messages.

#### b. Failure of a downstream delivery channel (e.g., email API is temporarily down)

1.  **Outbound Message Queues as Buffers and Retriers:**
    *   Instead of directly calling the Email/Push/In-App APIs, the Notification Processing Service places messages into dedicated Outbound Message Queues (e.g., SQS, or separate Kafka topics).
    *   These queues act as buffers, absorbing spikes and decoupling our service from the delivery channels.
    *   **SQS Example:** If the Email Service is down, messages for email notifications will accumulate in the Email SQS queue. SQS consumers (the Email Service's workers) will attempt to process them, fail, and the messages will become visible again after a `VisibilityTimeout`. This allows for automatic retries. If repeated retries fail, messages can be moved to a Dead-Letter Queue (DLQ) for manual inspection and reprocessing.
    *   **Kafka Example:** If the Email Service's Kafka consumers fail to process a message, they can either commit the offset and rely on their own internal retry logic, or not commit the offset, causing the message to be re-read. More commonly, a separate retry topic or DLQ pattern is used with Kafka.
2.  **Circuit Breakers (Optional but Recommended):**
    *   The Notification Processing Service itself might implement circuit breakers when deciding to put a message into an outbound queue. If the Email Service has been consistently failing (as observed by the Email Service's workers failing to consume from its queue, or by health checks), the Notification Processing Service could temporarily "open the circuit" for email notifications.
    *   When the circuit is open, instead of sending to the Email Outbound Queue, notifications could be routed to an alternative (e.g., an in-app notification if available) or a special "failed email" queue for later manual review, preventing our service from contributing to the problem.
3.  **Monitoring and Alerting:**
    *   Robust monitoring on the Outbound Message Queues (e.g., queue depth, age of oldest message, number of messages in DLQ) and the Delivery Services themselves is critical.
    *   Alerts trigger when thresholds are crossed, notifying on-call engineers to investigate and resolve issues with the downstream delivery channels.

This layered approach ensures that a temporary outage of a delivery channel does not lead to lost notifications. Instead, notifications are safely queued and retried, preserving the user experience as much as possible once the downstream service recovers.

### Going further

#### Trade-offs of chosen hot spot approach (partitioning by `user_id`) vs. round-robin

**Chosen Approach: Partitioning by `user_id`**

*   **Pros:**
    *   **Guaranteed Ordering per User:** All notifications for a single user are processed sequentially within a partition, which is crucial for maintaining logical consistency (e.g., "cart expires" before "cart purchased").
    *   **Isolation of Hot Users:** A hot user only impacts the processing of their specific partition, not the entire system. Other users' notifications continue to flow unimpeded.
    *   **Simpler Deduplication:** Deduplication logic for a specific user/event can rely on the fact that messages for that user are processed in order, simplifying state management.
    *   **Scalability:** By increasing the number of partitions and consumer instances, throughput can be scaled horizontally.

*   **Cons:**
    *   **Uneven Partition Distribution:** If there are a few extremely "hot" users, their partitions might become bottlenecks. While other partitions are processed quickly, the hot partitions could still cause latency for those specific users. This leads to uneven resource utilization across workers.
    *   **Limited Parallelism for Hot Users:** A single partition can only be consumed by one worker instance at a time (in Kafka/Kinesis consumer groups). This means a single hot user cannot benefit from parallel processing beyond the single worker assigned to their partition.
    *   **Rebalancing Overhead:** When workers join or leave the consumer group, partitions need to be re-assigned, which can cause a brief pause in processing.

**Alternative: Simple Round-Robin Distribution**

*   **Pros:**
    *   **Even Load Distribution:** Messages are distributed very evenly across all available workers, maximizing parallelism and resource utilization *on average*.
    *   **Simplicity:** Easier to implement initially as it doesn't require complex partitioning key logic.

*   **Cons:**
    *   **Loss of Ordering:** Notifications for the same user could be processed by different workers concurrently or out of order, leading to logical inconsistencies (e.g., a "cart purchased" notification being processed before a "cart expires" notification, if the "expires" message was delayed).
    *   **Hot Spot Amplification:** A hot user or event type could still overwhelm *any* worker it lands on. If a worker is processing a burst for one user, it might delay messages for other users that landed on the same worker via round-robin. The problem isn't isolated; it spreads.
    *   **Complex Deduplication:** Deduplication becomes harder. If messages for the same user/event can arrive at different workers, ensuring "exactly once" requires more robust distributed locking or coordination beyond a simple `SET ... NX` on a single key.

**Under what circumstances would the chosen approach become problematic?**

The `user_id` partitioning approach becomes problematic if:

1.  **Extremely Skewed User Activity:** If a very small number of users (e.g., 0.1% of users) generate a disproportionately massive percentage of the total notification volume (e.g., 50% of messages), their dedicated partitions will become extreme bottlenecks. Even with dedicated workers, a single partition's throughput limit might be hit, leading to latency for those specific users.
2.  **Insufficient Number of Partitions:** If the number of partitions is too low, the granularity of isolation is reduced. Many hot users might end up in the same partition, effectively creating a "super hot" partition.
3.  **Very Large Messages:** If messages themselves are very large, even a moderate number of messages in a single partition can overwhelm a worker's memory or network bandwidth.

**What alternative would I consider then?**

If `user_id` partitioning proves insufficient due to extreme skew, I would consider a **composite partitioning strategy** or a **two-tier queuing system**:

1.  **Composite Partitioning:** Instead of just `user_id`, use a combination like `hash(user_id + event_type)`. This increases the cardinality of the partitioning key, potentially distributing hot users more widely if different event types for the same user are distinct enough. However, it sacrifices strict per-user ordering if `event_type` is part of the key.

2.  **Two-Tier Queuing System (or "Hot Partition" Strategy):**
    *   **Tier 1 (Current Design):** Use `user_id` partitioning as described.
    *   **Tier 2 (Hot Partition Handling):** Implement a mechanism to detect "hot partitions" (e.g., partitions with consistently high lag or message rates).
    *   When a partition is identified as hot, messages from that partition could be temporarily routed to a *secondary, dedicated "hot partition" queue* with a different, more granular partitioning strategy (e.g., `user_id + notification_id` or even round-robin for that specific hot stream if ordering is less critical).
    *   This secondary queue would have its own set of highly scaled, specialized workers. This allows for dynamic re-routing of problematic streams without affecting the stable majority.
    *   This is significantly more complex to implement and manage, but it offers the flexibility to apply different processing strategies to different load profiles.

For our scenario, with a 5-minute latency budget and the nature of marketing notifications, the `user_id` partitioning is generally robust. The "extremely skewed user activity" scenario is less common for *marketing notifications* than, say, real-time gaming events. If a user is truly generating hundreds of thousands of marketing notifications per minute, there's likely an upstream data quality issue that needs to be addressed first. The `user_id` partitioning helps surface such issues by making the specific hot user's latency visible.
